    The Geography of the Local Service Multiplier

    Job market paper. Draft: November 2026.

    • Paper
    Abstract

    State and local governments spend approximately $20 billion a year on firm-specific subsidies, rationalized by the direct jobs these firms create and the indirect jobs their payroll supports. This paper studies, conditional on firm entry, the strength of the local service multiplier and how it depends on commuting and shopping networks in a city. Using employer-employee linked Census data, I find that about half of the entrant’s new employees were not previously attached to the city labor market. Whether these workers migrate, commute from elsewhere, or were previously unemployed determines how much of the entrant’s payroll becomes new local spending. I build a spatial equilibrium model across neighborhoods, calibrated to these rates of migration, commuting, and labor force participation, in which residents’ choices of where to shop determine where their income is spent. In the model, the service multiplier is smaller than most existing estimates. For firm entries of the same relative size, the multiplier is about 2.7 times as large at the 90th percentile as at the 10th. Aggregate characteristics of the local economy explain only a small share of this variation, and much of the rest reflects the commuting and shopping flows around the entrant’s specific location. Finally, I combine the model with a new dataset of subsidized firm entry events that records promised jobs, realized jobs, and the state and local portions of each subsidy award. In most events, the added residential property tax from the entry covers the local share of the award, and residents’ welfare gains exceed the award’s full cost, assuming the firm would not have entered otherwise. However, the same subsidy can deliver substantially different welfare gains at other locations in the same city.

  • Who Benefits from Urban Place Based Policies? Evidence from Tax Increment Financing Districts

    with M. Chen and E. Milstein

    Abstract

    We build the first comprehensive national evaluation of tax increment financing (TIF), a widely used but contested urban place-based policy in which cities freeze a district’s assessed property value and divert incremental tax revenue into local development rather than general public services. Existing evidence is fragmented across single-city studies and narrow outcomes, leaving open whether TIFs generate new economic activity or merely relocate it, and who ultimately captures the gains. We assemble a new harmonized geospatial database of TIF districts covering the 100 largest U.S. cities and link it to CoreLogic data, public data, and Census microdata on the universe of firms and workers. Using staggered and stacked event-study designs around district adoption, we estimate effects on employment, residential property values, and service activity. We find meager employment growth within districts, but residential property valuation gains when projects support local amenities.
